Tutorial 5.3  –  Trolley Impacting a Spring

Problem Statement

A trolley of mass 1 kg moves at a speed of 3 m/s towards a spring of unknown stiffness. The trolley comes to a stop when the spring has deflected by 2 cm. Neglect frictional effects. Determine the stiffness required in the spring and the impact force on the spring.

(prob 2-46, Engineering Mechanics of Solids, Popov E.P., 1990, Prentice-Hall)

Given answer:   $k = 22.5\ \text{kN/m}$,   $F_e = 450\ \text{N}$

Solution

1
Choose the right principle: energy, not impulse
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 What the given data tells you to use

Look at what the problem gives you: a distance (2 cm), not a time. That single observation selects the method.

Rule of thumb for impact problems:

• Given a stopping time → use impulse–momentum, $F\Delta t = m\Delta v$  (see T5.1)

• Given a stopping distance or deflection → use work–energy, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = U$

Here the trolley's kinetic energy is entirely converted into strain energy stored in the spring, because friction is neglected and the motion is horizontal (so no change in gravitational potential energy):

$$\underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2}}_{\text{kinetic energy in}} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}k\delta^{2}}_{\text{strain energy stored}}$$

At the instant of maximum compression the trolley is momentarily at rest, so all kinetic energy has been transferred. That is the condition the problem describes.

2
Solve for the required stiffness k
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 Rearrange the energy balance — watch the cm → m conversion

Rearranging $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}k\delta^2$ (the halves cancel):

$$k = \frac{m v^{2}}{\delta^{2}}$$

Convert the deflection first: $\delta = 2\ \text{cm} = 0.02$ m. Then

$$k = \frac{1 \times (3)^{2}}{(0.02)^{2}} = \frac{9}{0.0004} = 22\,500\ \text{N/m}$$

$$k = 22\,500\ \text{N/m} = 22.5\ \text{kN/m}$$

Physical reading: 22.5 kN/m means 22.5 N of force per millimetre of compression. Note the strong sensitivity: $k \propto 1/\delta^2$. Allowing the spring to travel 4 cm instead of 2 cm would need only a quarter of the stiffness — and would halve the peak force. Softer and longer-travel is almost always the better impact-absorbing design.

3
Find the impact force on the spring
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 Peak force occurs at maximum deflection

A linear spring obeys $F = k x$, so the force is greatest when the compression is greatest — at $x = \delta = 0.02$ m, the instant the trolley stops:

$$F_e = k\,\delta = 22\,500 \times 0.02 = 450\ \text{N}$$

$$F_e = 450\ \text{N}$$

Energy cross-check: because the force builds linearly from 0 to $F_e$, the average force over the stroke is $F_e/2 = 225$ N. The work it does is

$$225 \times 0.02 = 4.5\ \text{J}$$

and the incoming kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}(1)(3)^2 = 4.5$ J. ✓ The two match exactly, confirming both $k$ and $F_e$.

Shortcut worth remembering: combining the two steps eliminates $k$ entirely — $F_e = mv^2/\delta$. Substituting: $1 \times 9 / 0.02 = 450$ N. Equivalently, $F_e = 2 \times (\text{KE}) / \delta$: peak force equals twice the energy divided by the stroke, for any linear spring.

4
Interpret the result: how severe is this impact?
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Deceleration and contact time — linking back to T5.1

The peak deceleration follows from Newton's second law:

$$a = \frac{F_e}{m} = \frac{450}{1} = 450\ \text{m/s}^2 = 45.9\,g$$

Although no time was given, we can now find one. A mass on a spring stops in exactly one quarter of a natural period:

$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{22\,500}{1}} = 150\ \text{rad/s}, \qquad \Delta t = \frac{\pi}{2\omega_n} = 0.0105\ \text{s}$$

Checking against the impulse–momentum result of T5.1, the average force over that time is

$$\bar{F} = \frac{m v}{\Delta t} = \frac{1 \times 3}{0.0105} = 286\ \text{N}$$

which is $2/\pi \approx 0.64$ times the peak of 450 N — exactly the mean-to-peak ratio of a quarter sine wave. ✓

Why this matters: impulse–momentum gives the average force; energy methods give the peak. Design against the peak. Using the average would under-predict the load by 36 % here.

Impact factor: the trolley's weight is only $mg = 9.81$ N, yet the spring sees 450 N — a factor of 46. As in T5.1, the lesson is that a component sized for the static weight of a moving object is nowhere near adequate.

Common mistake 1: Leaving $\delta$ in centimetres. Substituting $\delta = 2$ gives $k = 2.25$ N/m — off by 10 000, since $\delta$ is squared. Convert to metres before substituting, not afterwards.

Common mistake 2: Writing $F_e = \tfrac{1}{2}k\delta$ or $F_e = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2/\delta = 225$ N. The factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ belongs to the energy expressions, not to the force. The spring force at full compression is the full $k\delta$; 225 N is the average force during the stroke, not the peak.

Common mistake 3: Trying $F = mv/\Delta t$ without a given $\Delta t$, then inventing one. No time is supplied — that is the deliberate signal to use energy. (The 10.5 ms in Step 4 was derived from $k$, not assumed.)

Common mistake 4: Including a $mgh$ term. The trolley moves horizontally, so gravitational potential energy does not change. That term appears only for dropped or vertically-moving masses (T5.5, T5.6).
