Tutorial 5.1  –  Impact Force from a Dropped Ball

Problem Statement

Calculate the impact force when a steel ball of mass 25 kg is dropped from a height of 6.5 m. It takes 10 ms for the steel ball to come to a complete stop.

Given answer:   $F_e = -28.25\ \text{kN}$

Solution

1
Understand what "impact force" actually means here
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 Momentum change over contact time — not weight

The ball's weight is only $mg = 25 \times 9.81 = 245$ N. Yet the answer is over 28 000 N — more than 100 times larger. The reason is that impact is a momentum problem, not a statics problem.

During free fall the ball accumulates momentum $p = mv$. The ground must destroy all of it, and it only has $\Delta t = 10$ ms to do so. The shorter the stopping time, the larger the force:

$$F\,\Delta t = \Delta p = m\,\Delta v \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad F = \frac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t}$$

Design insight: $F \propto 1/\Delta t$. Everything engineers use to soften an impact — crumple zones, rubber mounts, packaging foam, a diver bending their knees — works by stretching $\Delta t$. It cannot change the momentum that must be removed; it only changes how quickly.

So the solution has exactly two parts: (1) find the velocity at impact, (2) divide the momentum change by the contact time.

2
Impact velocity from the drop height
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 Energy conservation during free fall

The ball is released from rest and falls freely, so all of its potential energy converts to kinetic energy just before contact:

$$mgh = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v = \sqrt{2gh}$$

Note that the mass cancels — the impact speed is independent of mass (Galileo's result). Substituting $g = 9.81$ m/s² and $h = 6.5$ m:

$$v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 6.5} = \sqrt{127.53} = 11.29\ \text{m/s}$$

$$v = 11.29\ \text{m/s} \quad (\approx 40.7\ \text{km/h})$$

Sanity check: a 6.5 m drop is roughly a two-storey fall, and 40 km/h is a believable terminal speed for it. If your answer came out in the hundreds, you probably forgot the square root.

3
Apply the impulse–momentum principle
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 F = mΔv/Δt with consistent units and signs

Take downward as positive. The ball enters contact at $v_1 = +11.29$ m/s and leaves at $v_2 = 0$ (it comes to a complete stop):

$$\Delta v = v_2 - v_1 = 0 - 11.29 = -11.29\ \text{m/s}$$

Convert the contact time before substituting: $\Delta t = 10\ \text{ms} = 0.010$ s.

$$F_e = \frac{m\,\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{25 \times (-11.29)}{0.010}$$
$$F_e = -28\,232\ \text{N} = -28.23\ \text{kN}$$

$$F_e = -28.2\ \text{kN} \quad \text{(negative = acting upward, opposing the motion)}$$

What the minus sign means: it is not a compressive-stress convention. It simply says the force points opposite to the chosen positive (downward) direction — i.e. the ground pushes up on the ball to decelerate it. By Newton's third law the ball pushes down on the ground with 28.2 kN.

On the textbook's 28.25 kN: the book rounds $v$ to 11.3 m/s before the final division, giving $25 \times 11.3 / 0.01 = 28\,250$ N exactly. Carrying $v = 11.2929$ m/s through gives 28.23 kN. The 0.07 % gap is pure rounding, not a method difference. [Confidence: high — the discrepancy reproduces exactly when 11.3 is substituted.]

4
Should the ball's weight be included during contact?
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Why gravity is negligible in the impulse balance

Strictly, two forces act on the ball while it is in contact: the ground reaction $N$ (up) and its weight $mg$ (down). The rigorous impulse balance is:

$$(N - mg)\,\Delta t = m\,|\Delta v| \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad N = \frac{m|\Delta v|}{\Delta t} + mg$$
$$N = 28\,232 + 245 = 28\,477\ \text{N} = 28.48\ \text{kN}$$

The weight adds only 0.87 %. This is why impact analyses routinely drop gravity during the contact phase: over milliseconds, the gravitational impulse $mg\,\Delta t = 2.45$ N·s is tiny next to the momentum change $m|\Delta v| = 282$ N·s.

When it stops being negligible: if the contact time were long (say 0.5 s, a soft mat), the gravitational impulse grows to 123 N·s against a momentum change of 282 N·s — no longer ignorable. The rule of thumb is that gravity matters when $\Delta t$ approaches $v/g \approx 1.15$ s here.

Impact factor: $28\,232 / 245 = \mathbf{115}$. The ground carries 115 times the ball's dead weight. This ratio is exactly what a designer must guard against — a mounting sized for the static 245 N would fail instantly.

Common mistake 1: Leaving $\Delta t$ in milliseconds. Substituting $\Delta t = 10$ instead of $0.010$ gives 28.2 N — off by a factor of 1000, and suspiciously close to the weight, which makes the error easy to miss. Always convert to seconds first.

Common mistake 2: Using $F = mg$ or $F = ma$ with $a = g$. That is the free-fall force, valid only while the ball is falling. During contact the deceleration is $a = v/\Delta t = 1129$ m/s² ≈ 115 g — that is the number that produces the large force.

Common mistake 3: Using the drop height directly in an energy balance, e.g. $F = mgh/\Delta t$. Energy methods need a stopping distance, not a stopping time. Since only a time is given here, the impulse–momentum route is the correct one.

Common mistake 4: Reporting the sign carelessly. Quote the magnitude 28.2 kN and state the direction in words; a bare "−28.2 kN" is only meaningful once the positive direction is declared.
