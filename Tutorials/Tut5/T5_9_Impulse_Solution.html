Tutorial 5.9  –  Impulse when Stepping off a Wall

Problem Statement

Calculate the impulse when a person with a weight of 80 kg steps off a 50 cm high wall. They bend their knees so that the downwards motion is stopped and the contact time with the ground is 0.3 seconds.

Given answer:   Impulse $= 250.6$ N·s

Solution

1
What "impulse" means, and what it does not need
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 Impulse = change in momentum

Impulse is defined as the integral of force over time, and by Newton's second law that equals the change in momentum:

$$J = \int F\,dt = \Delta p = m\,\Delta v$$

Read the question carefully: it asks for the impulse, not the force. The impulse is fixed the moment the person leaves the wall — it depends only on the momentum they arrive with. The 0.3 s contact time does not change it. Bending the knees changes how the impulse is delivered (a smaller force over a longer time), never how large it is.

So the 0.3 s is not needed for the answer as asked — it is there so you can go on to find the force, which we do in Step 4. Recognising which given data a question actually needs is part of the skill being tested.

Units: $J$ is in N·s, which is dimensionally identical to kg·m/s. Both are correct; N·s emphasises the force–time reading, kg·m/s the momentum reading.

2
Landing velocity
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 Stepping off means starting from rest

"Steps off" — not jumps off — so the initial vertical velocity is zero and the fall is free:

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.5} = \sqrt{9.81} = 3.132\ \text{m/s}$$

$$v = 3.13\ \text{m/s} \quad (\approx 11.3\ \text{km/h})$$

Neat coincidence: with $h = 0.5$ m, $2gh = g$ numerically, so $v = \sqrt{g} = 3.132$ m/s. Useful as a memory anchor: a half-metre drop always lands you at about 3.1 m/s, roughly jogging pace.

3
Compute the impulse
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 One multiplication

The person arrives at 3.132 m/s and finishes at rest, so the magnitude of the momentum change is:

$$J = m\,\Delta v = 80 \times 3.132 = 250.6\ \text{kg·m/s}$$

$$J = 250.6\ \text{N·s} \quad \text{(directed upward, opposing the fall)}$$

Combined form: $J = m\sqrt{2gh}$ — impulse scales linearly with mass but only with the square root of drop height. Stepping off a 1 m wall instead of 0.5 m increases the impulse by just 41 %, not 100 %.

4
What the 0.3 s is for: the landing force
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Why bending your knees works

The average force required to deliver that impulse over 0.3 s:

$$\bar{F} = \frac{J}{\Delta t} = \frac{250.6}{0.3} = 835\ \text{N}$$

This is the net (decelerating) force. The ground must also support the person's weight throughout the landing, so the actual average ground reaction is:

$$N = \frac{J}{\Delta t} + mg = 835 + 785 = 1620\ \text{N} = 2.06 \times \text{body weight}$$

The point of the exercise: compare this with a stiff-legged landing, where the contact time might be 0.05 s:

$$N = \frac{250.6}{0.05} + 785 = 5012 + 785 = 5797\ \text{N} = 7.4 \times \text{body weight}$$

Same impulse, same drop, same person — but 3.6 times the force, purely from the shorter contact time. Bending the knees is a biological crumple zone, and it works for exactly the reason a car's crumple zone does: $F = J/\Delta t$, so stretch $\Delta t$.

Connecting the tutorial: compare T5.1, where a 25 kg ball stopped in 10 ms generated 115 times its own weight. Here a heavier person stopping over 0.3 s — 30 times longer — sees only 2 times body weight. The impulse $J$ is set by the fall; everything after that is a design choice about $\Delta t$.

Common mistake 1: Dividing by the contact time and reporting 835 N as "the impulse". That is a force, in newtons. Impulse is force × time, in N·s. The question asks for the quantity that does not depend on how you land.

Common mistake 2: Using $J = mgh = 392$ N·s or $J = mg\Delta t = 235$ N·s. The first is an energy (joules); the second is only the gravitational impulse during contact, not the momentum change.

Common mistake 3: Using $v = 2gh$ instead of $\sqrt{2gh}$, giving 9.81 m/s and an impulse of 785 N·s. Check the units: $2gh$ has units of m²/s², i.e. velocity squared.

Common mistake 4: Forgetting to add $mg$ when the question asks for the ground reaction rather than the net decelerating force. The two differ by 785 N here — nearly a factor of two on the answer.

Common mistake 5: Assuming the person jumps rather than steps. "Steps off" means $v_0 = 0$; any downward push would add to $v$ and hence to $J$.
