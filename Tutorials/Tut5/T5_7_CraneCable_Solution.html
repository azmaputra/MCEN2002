Tutorial 5.7  –  Crane Cable: Sudden Braking of a Descending Load

Problem Statement

A crane was lowering a load (W = 2250 kg) at a uniform rate of 0.6 m/s when the brakes on the drum were suddenly applied. Assuming that the upper end of the cable stopped instantaneously, find the maximum force induced in the cable. Include the effect of static load. Take $E = 110$ GPa and a cross-sectional area of $A = 320$ mm² for the twisted wire cable, with a free length of 6.1 m. The static deflection is given as $\delta_{st} = \dfrac{mgL}{AE}$.

(ex. 12.19, Design of Machine Elements, Spotts M.F., Third Edition, Prentice-Hall)

Given answer:   $F_{max} = 90.44$ kN

Solution

1
Identify what makes this problem different
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 A moving supported load, not a falling free one

In T5.2, T5.5 and T5.6 a weight fell freely onto a member that carried nothing beforehand. Here the situation is different in a way that changes the formula:

• The cable is already loaded — it carries $W$ and is already stretched by $\delta_{st}$ before the brakes are applied.

• The load is already moving at 0.6 m/s — no drop height is involved.

• The brake removes the support velocity instantaneously, so the cable must absorb the load's kinetic energy through additional stretch $\delta_a$ beyond $\delta_{st}$.

Write the energy balance over the additional stretch. The load loses kinetic energy and descends a further $\delta_a$ (so gravity does work $W\delta_a$); the cable's strain energy grows from $\tfrac{1}{2}k\delta_{st}^2$ to $\tfrac{1}{2}k(\delta_{st} + \delta_a)^2$:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} + W\delta_a = \tfrac{1}{2}k(\delta_{st} + \delta_a)^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}k\delta_{st}^{2}$$

Expand the right-hand side: $\tfrac{1}{2}k\delta_a^2 + k\delta_{st}\delta_a$. But $k\delta_{st} = W$ exactly, so that second term is $W\delta_a$ — and it cancels the work done by gravity on the left:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}k\delta_a^{2} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \delta_a = v\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

This cancellation is the whole insight. Because the cable is pre-tensioned to exactly balance the weight, gravity does no net work during the extra stretch — the strain energy it buys is exactly the potential energy it releases. The kinetic energy alone determines the additional deflection. This is why the answer is not $n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + v^2/g\delta_{st}}$, which would give 93.9 kN; that formula belongs to a load that is not yet supported.

2
Cable stiffness and static deflection
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 The cable as an axial spring

Working in N and mm ($E = 110\,000$ N/mm², $L = 6100$ mm), with $W = mg = 2250 \times 9.81 = 22\,072.5$ N:

$$k = \frac{AE}{L} = \frac{320 \times 110\,000}{6100} = 5770.5\ \text{N/mm}$$
$$\delta_{st} = \frac{W}{k} = \frac{22\,072.5}{5770.5} = 3.825\ \text{mm}$$

On the modulus: 110 GPa is well below steel's 200 GPa. That is deliberate — a twisted wire rope is not a solid bar. Under load the helical strands tighten and straighten, adding compliance, so ropes are quoted with an effective or apparent modulus around half that of the parent wire. Using 200 GPa here would under-predict $\delta_{st}$ and over-predict the force by about 35 %.

3
Additional deflection and dynamic force
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 Kinetic energy converts to extra stretch

In SI units, $k = 5.7705 \times 10^{6}$ N/m:

$$\delta_a = v\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 0.6\sqrt{\frac{2250}{5.7705\times10^{6}}} = 0.6 \times 0.019746 = 0.01185\ \text{m}$$
$$\delta_a = 11.85\ \text{mm}$$

The extra force this stretch generates is:

$$F_{dyn} = k\,\delta_a = 5770.5 \times 11.85 = 68\,367\ \text{N} = 68.4\ \text{kN}$$

$$F_{dyn} = 68.4\ \text{kN}$$

Equivalent one-line form: substituting $\delta_a = v\sqrt{m/k}$ into $F = k\delta_a$ gives

$$F_{dyn} = v\sqrt{mk} = 0.6\sqrt{2250 \times 5.7705\times10^{6}} = 68\,367\ \text{N} \ \checkmark$$

Worth memorising: the dynamic force from a moving mass brought to rest by a spring is $v\sqrt{mk}$ — the same expression that appeared in T5.3 and T5.4.

4
Add the static load — the total cable force
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Superposition: the cable was never unloaded

The maximum cable force is the force at maximum total stretch $\delta_{st} + \delta_a$:

$$F_{max} = k(\delta_{st} + \delta_a) = W + F_{dyn} = 22\,072.5 + 68\,367$$

$$F_{max} = 90\,440\ \text{N} = 90.44\ \text{kN}$$

The corresponding cable stress and impact factor:

$$\sigma_{max} = \frac{F_{max}}{A} = \frac{90\,440}{320} = 283\ \text{MPa}, \qquad n = \frac{F_{max}}{W} = \frac{90\,440}{22\,072.5} = 4.10$$

What a designer takes from this: a lowering speed of 0.6 m/s — a slow, unremarkable rate — multiplies the cable tension by 4.1 the instant the brake grabs. Crane ropes are specified with factors of safety of 5 or more precisely because of events like this, and why brakes are designed to ramp rather than to bite. Note that a longer cable would help: $k \propto 1/L$, so paying out more rope softens the system and reduces $F_{dyn} = v\sqrt{mk}$ by $\sqrt{L}$.

5
Why not the usual impact factor?
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 5 The 3.8 % trap — and when each formula applies

A very common approach is to reach for the velocity form of the standard impact factor:

$$n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{v^{2}}{g\,\delta_{st}}} = 1 + \sqrt{1 + \frac{0.36}{9.81 \times 0.003825}} = 4.255$$
$$F = nW = 4.255 \times 22\,072.5 = 93.9\ \text{kN} \quad \text{(3.8\% too high)}$$

That formula is derived for a mass moving at $v$ that then engages an unloaded spring — the weight's work $W\delta$ is genuinely new energy going into the member. Here the cable was already carrying the weight, so that term was already accounted for in $\delta_{st}$ and must not be counted twice.

Which formula, when:

• Load not yet supported by the member (dropped weight, free fall through $h$):   $n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + 2h/\delta_{st}}$, or with initial velocity, $n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + v^2/g\delta_{st}}$.

• Load already supported and moving (this problem, lifts, hoists, suddenly braked drives):   $F_{max} = W + v\sqrt{mk}$.

Direction of the error: in this case the wrong formula happens to be conservative (93.9 > 90.4 kN), so it would not cause an unsafe design — but it is still the wrong physics, and the problem explicitly instructs "include the effect of static load", which is the hint that superposition is intended.

Common mistake 1: Forgetting to add the static load. Reporting only $F_{dyn} = 68.4$ kN under-predicts the cable force by 24 % — an unsafe error, and the reason the question says "include the effect of static load".

Common mistake 2: Using $n = 1 + \sqrt{1 + v^2/g\delta_{st}}$ (Step 5). It gives 93.9 kN because it double-counts the work done by the weight, which is already embedded in $\delta_{st}$.

Common mistake 3: Using $E = 200$ GPa "because it's steel". A twisted wire rope has an effective modulus roughly half that of solid steel wire; the question supplies 110 GPa for exactly this reason.

Common mistake 4: Mixing N–mm and SI inside $\delta_a = v\sqrt{m/k}$. With $v$ in m/s and $m$ in kg, $k$ must be in N/m ($5.77\times10^6$), not N/mm. Using 5770.5 here gives an answer 31.6 times too large.

Common mistake 5: Treating "the upper end stopped instantaneously" as meaning the load also stops instantaneously. The load keeps moving for a further 11.85 mm — that stretch is precisely what generates the force.
