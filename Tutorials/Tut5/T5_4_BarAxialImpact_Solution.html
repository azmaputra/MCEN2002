Tutorial 5.4  –  Axial Impact on a Steel Bar (with Mass Correction)

Problem Statement

A 2.5 cm diameter round steel bar, 0.5 m long, is struck by an 18 kg weight moving at a speed of 0.8 m/s. The static deflection of the bar is given as $\delta_{bar} = \dfrac{mgL}{AE}$. Assume $E = 200$ GPa and density $\rho = 7850$ kg/m³.

a) Find the stress due to the impact assuming no dissipation of energy.
b) Find the stress due to the impact taking into account the dissipation of energy due to the differences in weight.

(ex. 8, Design of Machine Elements, Spotts M.F., Third Edition, Prentice-Hall)

Given answer:   a) $\sigma = 96.9$ MPa    b) $\sigma = 95.2$ MPa

Solution

1
Section properties and the bar as a spring
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 A, k = AE/L, and why that is the whole model

Cross-sectional area of the round bar:

$$A = \frac{\pi d^{2}}{4} = \frac{\pi (25)^{2}}{4} = 490.9\ \text{mm}^{2} = 4.909 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^{2}$$

The given static deflection formula $\delta_{bar} = \dfrac{mgL}{AE}$ is just Hooke's law written for an axially loaded bar, $\delta = FL/AE$. Rearranged, it tells us the bar behaves as a linear spring of stiffness

$$k = \frac{F}{\delta} = \frac{AE}{L} = \frac{(4.909\times10^{-4})(200\times10^{9})}{0.5} = 1.963\times10^{8}\ \text{N/m}$$

Why this matters: once you see $k = AE/L$, T5.4 becomes structurally identical to T5.3 (trolley into a spring) — same energy balance, different $k$. The only new ingredient is converting the resulting force into a stress. Note how stiff the bar is: 196 MN/m, about 8700 times the trolley spring. Stiff members make bad shock absorbers.

2
Part (a): energy balance with no dissipation
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 Kinetic energy in = strain energy stored

The weight arrives horizontally with kinetic energy

$$KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(18)(0.8)^{2} = 5.76\ \text{J}$$

Assuming none of it is lost, all of it becomes elastic strain energy in the bar. Strain energy of an axially loaded bar can be written either in terms of deflection or directly in terms of stress:

$$U = \tfrac{1}{2}k\delta^{2} = \frac{\sigma^{2}AL}{2E}$$

Equating $KE = U$ using the stress form and solving for $\sigma$:

$$\tfrac{1}{2}mv^{2} = \frac{\sigma^{2}AL}{2E} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \sigma = v\sqrt{\frac{mE}{AL}}$$

Substituting in consistent SI units:

$$\sigma = 0.8\sqrt{\frac{18 \times 200\times10^{9}}{(4.909\times10^{-4})(0.5)}} = 0.8\sqrt{1.467\times10^{16}}$$
$$\sigma = 0.8 \times 1.211\times10^{8} = 9.689\times10^{7}\ \text{Pa}$$

$$\sigma_a = 96.9\ \text{MPa}$$

Equivalent route via deflection (use whichever you find clearer — same answer):

$$\delta = v\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} = 0.8\sqrt{\frac{18}{1.963\times10^{8}}} = 0.2422\ \text{mm}$$

$$F = k\delta = 47\,560\ \text{N}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{47\,560}{4.909\times10^{-4}} = 96.9\ \text{MPa} \ \checkmark$$

Scale of the effect: the static stress from simply resting the weight on the bar is $\sigma_{st} = mg/A = 176.6/4.909\times10^{-4} = 0.36$ MPa. The impact multiplies it by 269. Yet 96.9 MPa is still comfortably below the yield strength of structural steel (~250 MPa), so the elastic assumption behind $U = \sigma^2 AL/2E$ holds. ✓

3
Part (b): why energy is lost when the bar has mass
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 The physical mechanism — a plastic collision, not friction

Part (a) assumed the bar was massless. In reality the striker does not meet a weightless spring; it meets a body with inertia that must be suddenly accelerated from rest to the striker's speed. That sudden common-velocity event is a perfectly inelastic collision, and such collisions always lose kinetic energy.

Note the wording: "dissipation of energy due to the differences in weight" — the loss has nothing to do with friction, damping, or heat in the usual sense. It is the momentum-sharing loss of a plastic impact between the striker and the bar's effective mass.

First, the bar's actual mass:

$$m_{bar} = \rho A L = 7850 \times (4.909\times10^{-4}) \times 0.5 = 1.927\ \text{kg}$$

Not all of that mass moves at the impact velocity — the fixed end stays put and the struck end moves fastest. Integrating the kinetic energy of a bar with a linear velocity distribution gives the classical result that the bar behaves as though only one third of its mass sits at the struck end:

$$m_{eff} = \frac{m_{bar}}{3} = \frac{1.927}{3} = 0.642\ \text{kg}$$

Conservation of momentum for the plastic impact gives the common velocity $v' = \dfrac{mv}{m + m_{eff}}$, and the kinetic energy surviving the collision is

$$\eta = \frac{KE_{\text{after}}}{KE_{\text{before}}} = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{m_{bar}}{3m}}$$
$$\eta = \frac{1}{1 + \dfrac{1.927}{3 \times 18}} = \frac{1}{1 + 0.03568} = 0.9655$$

$$\eta = 0.966 \quad \text{(3.4\% of the incoming energy is lost)}$$

4
Part (b): apply the correction to the stress
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Stress scales with the square root of energy

The critical detail: from $U = \sigma^2 AL/2E$, the stress is proportional to $\sqrt{U}$. So the energy factor $\eta$ enters the stress as $\sqrt{\eta}$, not as $\eta$:

$$\sigma_b = \sigma_a\sqrt{\eta} = 96.89 \times \sqrt{0.9655} = 96.89 \times 0.9826$$

$$\sigma_b = 95.2\ \text{MPa}$$

Or, all in one expression:

$$\sigma_b = v\sqrt{\frac{mE}{AL}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \dfrac{m_{bar}}{3m}}}$$

Is the correction worth making? It reduces the stress by only 1.7 %, because the bar (1.93 kg) is light next to the striker (18 kg). The correction always reduces the predicted stress, so ignoring it is conservative — you over-predict the load, which is the safe direction to err. For hand design work with a light struck member, part (a) alone is defensible. The correction becomes important when $m_{bar}$ approaches or exceeds $3m$: for a striker of 1 kg on this same bar, $\eta$ would fall to 0.61 and the stress to 78 % of the uncorrected value.

Common mistake 1: Applying $\eta$ directly to the stress, i.e. $96.9 \times 0.9655 = 93.6$ MPa. Energy scales as $\sigma^2$, so the correction on stress is $\sqrt{\eta} = 0.9826$. The book's 95.2 MPa confirms the square root.

Common mistake 2: Using the full bar mass 1.927 kg instead of $m_{bar}/3 = 0.642$ kg. That would give $\eta = 0.903$ and 92.1 MPa. The one-third factor comes from integrating the kinetic energy of a bar whose velocity varies linearly from zero at the fixed end.

Common mistake 3: Adding a $mgh$ term. No drop height is given — the weight is already moving at 0.8 m/s when it makes contact, so its kinetic energy is the entire energy input. (Contrast T5.5, where a mass is released from a height and both $mgh$ and the work done through the extra deflection matter.)

Common mistake 4: Mixing mm and m mid-calculation. With $\sigma = v\sqrt{mE/AL}$ the units only work out if everything is SI: $A$ in m², $L$ in m, $E$ in Pa. Substituting $A = 490.9$ mm² alongside $E = 200 \times 10^9$ Pa gives an answer wrong by a factor of 1000.

Common mistake 5: Forgetting to check that the result is elastic. If the computed impact stress exceeded the yield strength, the linear strain-energy formula would be invalid and the whole calculation meaningless. Always compare against yield before reporting.
