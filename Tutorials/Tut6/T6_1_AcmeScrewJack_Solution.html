Tutorial 6.1  –  Acme Screw Jack: Torque, Efficiency & Self-Locking

Problem Statement

A screw jack with a 25 mm double Acme screw (pitch $p = 5$ mm) raises a load $W = 44\,480$ N. A plain thrust collar of mean diameter $d_c = 51$ mm is used. Running friction coefficients are $f = 0.13$ at the thread and $f_c = 0.10$ at the collar.

(a) Find the screw lead, thread depth, mean pitch diameter, and helix (lead) angle.  (b) Estimate the starting torque to raise and to lower the load.  (c) Estimate the efficiency when raising.  (d) Is the jack self-locking?

Given answers:  (a) $L = 10$ mm, thread depth $= 2.5$ mm, $d_m = 22.5$ mm, $\lambda = 8.05^\circ$   (b) $T_R = 315.15$ N·m, $T_L = 168.96$ N·m   (c) $27.87\%$   (d) No

Solution

1
Screw geometry: lead, thread depth, mean diameter, helix angle
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 1 Turn the given dimensions into the four geometric quantities

Lead is the axial advance per one full turn. A double (two-start) thread advances two pitches per turn:

$$L = n_{\text{starts}}\,p = 2 \times 5 = 10\text{ mm}$$

For an Acme thread the basic thread depth (and the amount by which the mean diameter sits below the major diameter) is half the pitch:

$$\text{depth} = \tfrac{p}{2} = \tfrac{5}{2} = 2.5\text{ mm} \qquad d_m = d - \tfrac{p}{2} = 25 - 2.5 = 22.5\text{ mm}$$

The helix (lead) angle $\lambda$ comes from unwrapping one turn of the thread at the mean radius — the rise $L$ over the circumference $\pi d_m$:

$$\lambda = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{L}{\pi d_m}\right) = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{10}{\pi \times 22.5}\right) = \tan^{-1}(0.1415) = 8.05^\circ$$

$$L = 10\text{ mm}, \quad \text{depth} = 2.5\text{ mm}, \quad d_m = 22.5\text{ mm}, \quad \lambda = 8.05^\circ$$

Why mean diameter? The thread force really acts spread over the flank, but its resultant is well modelled as acting at the mean radius $d_m/2$. Using the major diameter would overstate every torque; using the root would understate it.

2
Set up the Acme torque equations (thread + collar)
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 2 The raising / lowering torque formulas and the Acme angle correction

Total torque at the screw = thread torque + collar torque. For a thread with flank half-angle $\alpha$ (Acme: included angle $29^\circ$, so $\alpha = 14.5^\circ$), the friction on the flank is magnified by $\sec\alpha$ because the flank is tilted:

$$T_R = \underbrace{\frac{W d_m}{2}\left(\frac{L + \pi f d_m \sec\alpha}{\pi d_m - f L \sec\alpha}\right)}_{\text{raise the load (thread)}} + \underbrace{\frac{f_c W d_c}{2}}_{\text{collar}}$$
$$T_L = \frac{W d_m}{2}\left(\frac{\pi f d_m \sec\alpha - L}{\pi d_m + f L \sec\alpha}\right) + \frac{f_c W d_c}{2}$$

Starting vs. running friction. The problem gives running (kinetic) coefficients but asks for the starting torque. Static friction is higher — a standard estimate is $f_s \approx \tfrac{4}{3} f$. We therefore use $f_s = \tfrac{4}{3}(0.13) = 0.1733$ and $f_{c,s} = \tfrac{4}{3}(0.10) = 0.1333$ for the starting torque in part (b). Part (c) uses the running values because efficiency describes steady motion.

With $\alpha = 14.5^\circ$, $\sec\alpha = 1.0329$.

3
(b) Starting torque to raise the load
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 3 Substitute the static coefficients into $T_R$

Thread term, with $f_s = 0.1733$:

$$T_{R,\text{thread}} = \frac{44\,480 \times 22.5}{2}\left(\frac{10 + \pi (0.1733)(22.5)(1.0329)}{\pi (22.5) - (0.1733)(10)(1.0329)}\right)$$
$$= 500\,400 \times \frac{10 + 12.65}{70.69 - 1.79} = 500\,400 \times 0.3288 = 164\,540\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}$$

Collar term, with $f_{c,s} = 0.1333$:

$$T_{R,\text{collar}} = \frac{0.1333 \times 44\,480 \times 51}{2} = 151\,200\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}$$
$$T_R = 164\,540 + 151\,200 = 315\,700\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm} \approx 315.7\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

$$T_R \approx 315\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} \quad(\text{book } 315.15\text{ N}\cdot\text{m})$$

The collar carries ~48% of the torque. A plain thrust collar of 51 mm mean diameter is almost as big a friction source as the thread itself. Replacing it with a thrust bearing ($f_c \to \sim0.01$) would nearly halve the required torque.

4
(b) Starting torque to lower the load
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 4 Use the $T_L$ form (thread sign flips, collar unchanged)

Thread term:

$$T_{L,\text{thread}} = 500\,400 \times \frac{\pi(0.1733)(22.5)(1.0329) - 10}{\pi(22.5) + (0.1733)(10)(1.0329)} = 500\,400 \times \frac{12.65 - 10}{70.69 + 1.79} = 18\,320\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}$$

Collar term is the same as before ($151\,200$ N·mm — the collar doesn't care which way you turn):

$$T_L = 18\,320 + 151\,200 = 169\,500\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm} \approx 169.5\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

$$T_L \approx 169\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} \quad(\text{book } 168.96\text{ N}\cdot\text{m})$$

$T_L$ is still positive, meaning you must apply torque to lower — but that is almost entirely the collar contribution. The thread's own lowering torque ($18.3$ N·m) is small and positive here; that margin is what part (d) examines.

5
(c) Efficiency when raising
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 5 Compare useful work out to work in, per turn (running conditions)

Efficiency is the useful axial work $W L$ done per revolution divided by the input work $2\pi T_R$ per revolution. Efficiency describes steady operation, so use the running torque (with $f = 0.13$, $f_c = 0.10$). Re-evaluating $T_R$ with the running coefficients gives $T_{R,\text{run}} = 254.1$ N·m:

$$e = \frac{W L}{2\pi T_{R,\text{run}}} = \frac{44\,480 \times 0.010}{2\pi \times 254.1} = \frac{444.8}{1596.6} = 0.2786$$

$$e = 27.9\% \quad(\text{book } 27.87\%)$$

Low efficiency is normal — and often the point. Power screws routinely run at 15–40% efficiency; the "wasted" work is what makes the jack safe to leave under load. High efficiency and self-locking are mutually exclusive.

6
(d) Is it self-locking?
Try it first, then reveal ↓
Step 6 Test the thread alone: does friction beat the helix?

A screw is self-locking if, with the collar ignored, the load cannot drive the screw back down on its own — i.e. the thread's lowering torque is positive. The condition is:

$$f \sec\alpha \; \ge \; \tan\lambda$$

Using the given running coefficient $f = 0.13$:

$$f\sec\alpha = 0.13 \times 1.0329 = 0.1343 \qquad \tan\lambda = 0.1415$$
$$0.1343 \; < \; 0.1415 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{not self-locking}$$

No — the screw thread is not self-locking. On the thread alone the load would overhaul (back-drive).

Reconcile with part (b): $T_L$ came out positive only because the collar friction ($151$ N·m) masks the thread's tiny overhauling tendency. The self-locking test looks at the thread in isolation, so it is the thread condition $f\sec\alpha \ge \tan\lambda$ that decides. Here it fails by a whisker ($0.1343$ vs $0.1415$), so in practice the jack relies on the collar friction to hold — not a robust design margin.

Self-locking?—NoNo

Common mistake 1: Forgetting the two-start thread. A double thread has $L = 2p = 10$ mm, not $5$ mm. Using $L = 5$ halves $\tan\lambda$ and gives the wrong torque and the wrong self-locking conclusion.

Common mistake 2: Dropping the Acme $\sec\alpha$ factor. Treating the Acme thread as square ($\alpha = 0$) understates thread friction by ~3% and can flip a marginal self-locking judgement.

Common mistake 3: Using running friction for the "starting" torque. Starting torque needs the static coefficient ($f_s \approx \tfrac{4}{3}f$); using running values gives $T_R \approx 254$ N·m instead of $315$ N·m — a 20% underestimate of what the operator must actually apply to break the jack loose.

Common mistake 4: Including collar friction in the self-locking test. Self-locking is a property of the thread; the collar is a separate, order-dependent friction source.
